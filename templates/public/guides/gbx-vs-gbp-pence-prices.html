Two units, one currency

London prices come in two units:

  • GBP: pounds. A price of 4.52 means £4.52.
  • GBX (sometimes shown as GBp): pence. A price of 452 means £4.52.

Many London-listed ETFs and ETPs are quoted in pence. For example, the 3x Nasdaq-100 product LQQ3 is quoted in GBX, while the short-dated bond fund ERNS is quoted in GBP.

The 100× spreadsheet bug

Price feeds and spreadsheet functions often return the raw exchange price. If you hold 20 shares of a fund quoted at 25,000 GBX and multiply 20 × 25,000, your spreadsheet says £500,000. The real value is 20 × £250 = £5,000.

In a rebalancing plan the damage is worse than a silly total. It can tell you to sell almost everything, or to buy far too much, because the growth fund looks 100 times bigger than its target.

How to avoid it

  1. Look up the exact line you hold (ticker and exchange) and note its unit.
  2. Convert GBX prices to pounds once, in a single clearly labelled column, by dividing by 100.
  3. Sanity-check totals against your broker's valuation after every update.
  4. Watch for funds with several lines: the same fund can trade in USD, GBP and GBX under different tickers.

How Wealtharr handles it

Each instrument's quote unit is part of its fixed definition. Pence prices are converted before any valuation, and a price in an unexpected unit stops instructions rather than guessing.
