The idea in one paragraph

You hold two funds: a growth fund (for example a 3x Nasdaq-100 product) and a safe fund (for example a short-dated sterling bond fund or cash). You set a growth target for the growth fund, such as 9% a quarter. At each quarter end you compare where the growth fund actually is with where the target says it should be. If it's ahead, you sell the surplus into the safe fund. If it's behind, you buy the shortfall using the safe fund. That's the whole plan.

A worked quarter

Say you start with £10,000: £6,000 in growth and £4,000 in safe, a 60/40 split. With a 9% quarterly target, the growth fund's target for next quarter end is £6,000 × 1.09 = £6,540.

  • Growth ends the quarter at £7,200. It beat the target by £660. Sell about £660 of growth and buy £660 of safe. The growth fund restarts at £6,540.
  • Growth ends the quarter at £5,800. It missed the target by £740. Sell about £740 of safe and buy £740 of growth. The growth fund restarts at £6,540.

Either way, the next quarter's target is £6,540 × 1.09 ≈ £7,129, and the process repeats.

Adding new money

New deposits usually go into the safe fund or wait as cash, and part of them is added to the growth target, so the plan keeps its shape as your contributions grow. Record the exact date and amount of every deposit; it's what keeps the target honest.

Rules for rough markets

Because the plan buys when growth falls short, a long bear market can drain the safe fund. Common refinements include:

  • A crash rule: after a very deep fall in the broad market, skip the next few would-be sells so the recovery isn't sold too early.
  • A reset: when the safe fund runs out, or after a set time, return to the original split (such as 60/40) and restart the target from there.
  • Buy limits: cap a single quarter's buy so one bad quarter can't empty the safe fund.

Why people like it

  • It removes guesswork. The numbers decide; you just place the trades.
  • It's low effort. Four check-ins a year, plus whenever you add money.
  • It works with volatility. Swings turn into planned buy-low and sell-high trades. See volatility decay explained.

Where it goes wrong

Most failures are tracking mistakes, not the rules: a missed quarter end, a deposit recorded on the wrong date, or a price in pence treated as pounds. Our spreadsheet vs app checklist covers the usual traps.

Leveraged growth funds can fall very sharply, and no rebalancing rule removes that risk. The figures above are illustrations, not forecasts.
