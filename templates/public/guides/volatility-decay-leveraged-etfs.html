The two-day example

Take an index at 100. On day one it rises 10%; on day two it falls 10%. A 3x daily product starts at 100 too.

DayIndex moveIndex3x move3x product
Start—100.00—100.00
1+10%110.00+30%130.00
2−10%99.00−30%91.00

The index ends 1% down. "Three times the index" would suggest about 3% down, but the product ends 9% down. That extra gap is volatility decay, sometimes called beta slippage.

Why it happens

A loss needs a bigger gain to recover: after a 30% fall you need about a 43% rise to get back to where you were. A daily-reset product applies its leverage to each day's move separately, so up-and-down swings compound against it. The bigger the daily swings, the bigger the drag.

It can also work for you

In a steady trend the same compounding helps. If an index rises 1% every day for five days, a 3x daily product gains about 15.9%, slightly more than three times the index's 5.1%. Decay hurts in choppy, sideways markets; daily compounding helps in smooth trends.

What it means in practice

  • Long holding periods are unpredictable. Over a year, the product can return far more or far less than three times the index.
  • Sideways markets are expensive. An index that goes nowhere for a year can still leave a 3x product down noticeably.
  • Costs add up. On top of decay there are ongoing charges and the cost of the leverage itself.

How rules-based investors manage it

A common approach is to hold the leveraged fund as only one part of a portfolio, next to a steady "safe" fund, and rebalance on a fixed schedule. After strong runs, profits are moved to the safe side. After falls, the safe side buys more growth. That turns volatility into a source of rebalancing trades rather than just a drag. Quarterly signal rebalancing is one version of this.
